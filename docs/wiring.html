Nothing else joins the 5 V rail to VSYS or to 3V3. Those three are separate copper. The only link between 5 V and VSYS is through D1, and 3V3 is made by the Pico itself.
Check the SCD40 carrier before soldering it in. The Sensirion die itself takes 2.4–5.5 V, so 3.3 V suits it — but some AliExpress carriers put a 5 V→3.3 V regulator in front, and feeding one of those 3.3 V lands it below its dropout. Look for a small three-pin regulator next to the input pins. If there is one, the board wants 5 V — and then its pull-ups put 5 V on SDA and SCL, which the RP2040 will not tolerate on a shared bus.

Digit data line

The RGBDigit datasheet, read after the digits were already working, contradicts two things we had settled.

No external resistor on the digit data line. The module already carries 330 Ω in its Di line — the datasheet says so outright: "an external resistor is not necessary". Our R2 would have put 800 Ω in series, and that is a plausible contributor to the digit fault that metered 470 Ω correctly and still would not pass data. The ring's R1 stays: that module has no resistor of its own.
The digit's logic threshold is above what the Pico can drive. VIH is 0.7 × VDD, so at VDD = 5 V a high must reach 3.5 V — and the RP2040 drives 3.3 V. On paper the margin is negative. One digit on a short breadboard lead works anyway; four soldered inside a box is where that stops being reliable. The SN74HCT125N in the parts box is the fix: power it at 5 V, GP7 into 1A, 1Y to digit 1 DIN, 1OE tied low, unused inputs to ground.

Buzzer driver

The UDB-05B is a magnetic transducer, not a piezo: 5 V rated, 30 mA, resonant at 2300 ± 400 Hz. Both numbers matter.

                     ● 5 V rail
                     │
                  ┌──┴──┐
                  │ ⌒⌒⌒ │     UDB-05B · 30 mA · 2300 Hz
                  └──┬──┘
                     │
                     ●  collector
                    ╱
 pin 12 ──[ 1 kΩ ]──●  base        Q1 · 2N2222A (NPN)
  GP9        R8     ╲
                     ●  emitter
                     │

30 mA is over the RP2040's 12 mA per-pin rating. Driving the buzzer straight from GP9 runs the pin out of spec. The 2N2222A that turned out not to be needed for the arcade lamp goes here instead — and it lets the buzzer run at 5 V, which is where the datasheet's 83 dBA is measured. No code change: PWM drives a base perfectly well.
Melodies live in A6–F7 (1760–2794 Hz), straddling resonance. A magnetic buzzer off resonance is quiet and current-hungry — its impedance is mostly inductive reactance, which falls with frequency, so low notes draw more current to make less noise. The first version of these melodies sat at 260–1046 Hz, one to three octaves too low.

Power

The onboard regulator supplies 3.3 V on pin 36 for the LCD, the SCD40 and the encoder in both arrangements below. What changes is where the 5 V comes from.

On the breadboard now

Everything through the Pico's own micro-USB connector — the Raspberry Pi USB-C adapter via a USB-C-to-micro-USB cable. VBUS (pin 40) distributes 5 V to the pixels, the gas sensor and the buzzer. One source, so nothing can back-feed anything.

In the finished box — panel USB-C

The Adafruit 4090 breakout gives the case a proper USB-C hole instead of a micro-USB cable poking out of it. That reintroduces a second supply, which is what the Schottky is for.

The diode is what lets both ports be plugged in at once. VSYS accepts anything from 1.8 to 5.5 V and the Pico's own VBUS reaches VSYS through an identical internal Schottky, so whichever source is higher wins and neither drives current back into the other. That means the box can stay powered from the panel while you reflash it over micro-USB.
Pin 40 must stay unconnected in that arrangement. Tying it to the 5 V rail would wire the two supplies straight together, bypassing the diode entirely — the one thing it exists to prevent.
3.3 V for the encoder, never 5 V. Its onboard pull-ups sit on CLK and DT, so whatever feeds + is what those signal lines idle at. The RP2040 is not 5 V tolerant.
The LCD also stays on 3.3 V for the same reason — its pull-ups share the I²C bus with the Pico's pins.

Solder order

Rails first, then one module at a time, powering up between each. A fault found after two joints is a fault you can see; found after forty, it is a hunt.

The one joint that has already bitten you: the 470 Ω to the digit DIN. It read 470 Ω on the meter and still would not pass data, because a probe tip pressed onto a pad makes a connection the solder does not. Tug-test that wire, and check continuity from the far end of the resistor to the digit pad itself — not to the pad it is supposed to be joined to.
